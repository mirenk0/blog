Populism's rise has produced a new political animal, the rightoid. He isn't conservatism's revival. He's its death rattle. He thinks of himself as a dissident, but everything he does stays inside the system he claims to hate, and the system has learned to use him. He is what happens when politics becomes content.

## Oppressed and triumphant at once

The rightoid never steps outside the existing paradigm. Every issue gets sorted into right versus left, Republican versus Democrat, and every road ends at the same place: vote for the populist right and cheer. He'll swear he's more radical than that, but it's where he always ends up.

Holding that position takes a remarkable immunity to cognitive dissonance. He moves between oppressed dissident and conquering Caesar without noticing the switch. The system is rigged, except when Trump wins, and then the victory is clean. We're freedom fighters under the boot of the globalists, except when Trump receives some foreign leader at the White House, and then it's proof of five-dimensional chess. Persecuted on Monday, all-powerful by Wednesday, and never troubled by the contradiction.

## The feeling of winning

He's a reactionary in the literal sense. He reacts. Images and emotions matter to him, not systems or theory. What he wants from a political victory isn't concrete gain. It's the feeling of having won. Whether mass deportations actually happen at scale matters less than the montage of memes and clips that makes them feel real. Feeling as though he's winning satisfies the hunger, the way a picture of a banquet might if you stared at it long enough.

So his politics is all surface: social media feeds and cable news, shadows on the wall of Plato's cave. Digging beneath the event might carry him off in the undertow of how power really works, and it's more comfortable to float. His view of the world is a collage of clips and headlines. One out-of-context video is enough to confirm what he already felt, whatever piles of evidence point the other way.

## Politics by boycott

He lives inside consumer culture, so his victories take consumer form. "Go woke, go broke" is the whole theory. When Bud Light ran a promotion with a trans influencer, the answer wasn't any change in law or institutions. It was a boycott. The boycott did hurt sales, which only proves the point. A brewery lost market share, nothing about the culture that produced the ad changed, and he went back to scrolling, satisfied. The pleasure of watching a corporation suffer stands in for political engagement. He's a spectator who thinks the cheering is the game.

Winning, for him, is rhetorical and aesthetic, not legislative. He'll celebrate when migrants are bussed to New York to own the libs, with no thought for what that does to the city, then howl when New York elects a democratic socialist mayor and predict Sharia and ruin by the first week. He'll claim membership of a multiracial MAGA movement and cheer for non-white candidates, then dismiss a rival cause as "brown-coded". He spends emotion like cash, chasing the next hit, and his consistency drains to nothing. What protects him from noticing is a near-total absence of self-reflection.

## Conspiracy as entertainment

The rightoid is a conspiracy theorist, but a curiously limited one. His suspicion is aimed at content, the faked video and the doctored image, never at the structure that produces it. It's skepticism as performance. He enjoys the sensation of seeing through things and the dissident status it gives him, which saves him the labour of working out where power actually sits.

That's why his villains are so vague: the globalists, the elites, the satanic paedophiles. Any theory worth holding should explain things and predict what happens next. His explain nothing. They're closed loops of suspicion that lead nowhere and ask nothing of him. For a man who feels powerless, they work as a painkiller, numbing the ache without treating it.

## Faces instead of structures

His thinking runs on personalities. He repeats ready-made phrases like "Biden's America" and "Sadiq Khan's London" as if the name on the door explained the state of the building. The mayor of London has no power over immigration, which is set nationally. But a face is easier to hate than an apparatus, so the figurehead becomes a piñata, and he swings at it while the real machinery hums along untouched.

The same shallowness makes him easy to steer. Movements built to channel his anger into safe, pre-approved directions find him an ideal recruit. He'll take whatever enemy he's handed and run with it, because choosing his own would require independent thought.

## Aesthetics without power

He's obsessed with appearances, but as an end in themselves, not as a means to anything. He imagines some link between looks and power and feels entitled to mock any powerful person who falls short of his standard. His hero is an overweight pensioner with an orange tan, which doesn't seem to bother him. Coming from a man with no power at all, the habit is like a victim on the ground critiquing the footwear of the men kicking him.

## The pressure valve

In the end, the rightoid is a pressure-release valve. Real discontent with the status quo is growing, and he converts it into noise that changes nothing. He's coded as anti-establishment, but every move he makes is bounded by the establishment he thinks he's fighting. He's the system's immune response, harmless enough to be tolerated and loud enough to soak up energy that might otherwise go somewhere dangerous.

Real politics costs something. It means organisation, patience, institutions, theory, and years of unglamorous work with no clips to show for it. The rightoid won't pay that price. He takes the bait instead: memes, bluster, and outrage served up as an endless feed, a politics he plays like a role-playing game in which he is always the hero. Meanwhile the theatre he's performing in is on fire, and he's too busy filming himself to notice.
