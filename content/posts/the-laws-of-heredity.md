Most of what makes people different from each other is not taught, and it isn't chosen. It is inherited. That claim is still treated as taboo, but it is one of the best-replicated findings in all of social science. Behavioural genetics has reduced it to four laws, and together they cut against almost every assumption modern policy is built on.

## Everything is heritable

The first law: every human behavioural trait is heritable. Personality, intelligence, risk of mental illness, all of it. For any trait that varies between people, some of that variation comes from differences in their genes.

Heritable doesn't mean fixed. It means that genetic differences explain part of why one person ends up different from another. It is a statement about variation in a population, not a sentence passed on an individual. But it is true of every trait anyone has bothered to measure.

## The family matters less than you think

The second law is the one people resist most: being raised in the same family has a smaller effect than genes. Siblings who grow up together resemble each other mostly because they share DNA, not because they share a house, a dinner table, or a parenting style.

For most measured traits, the shared family environment comes out surprisingly close to zero. Adopted children raised side by side end up not much more alike than strangers. That is a hard pill for a culture that blames, credits, and tries to engineer outcomes through parenting, schooling, and household income.

## The rest is noise

The third law: a large share of the variation is explained by neither genes nor the shared family. What's left is the nonshared environment, meaning the experiences that differ even between siblings, plus plain measurement error.

That's why identical twins, with the same genome and the same upbringing, can still differ in personality, interests, and mental health. But this residue is mostly idiosyncratic: a particular illness, a particular friend, chance events in development. It isn't the kind of thing a policy can reach and pull on. Whatever isn't genetic is largely luck.

## Thousands of tiny dials

The fourth law: behavioural traits are shaped by many genes, each with a tiny effect. There is no gene for intelligence or for depression. There are thousands of variants, each nudging the outcome by a fraction of a percent.

That's why the old "candidate gene" studies, which hunted for single genes with big effects, almost never replicated. It took genome-wide studies of hundreds of thousands of people to find reliable signals.

Those studies produced a new tool: the polygenic score. Take millions of genetic markers, weight each one by how strongly it predicts a trait, and add them up. The result is a single number estimating a person's genetic predisposition for height, educational attainment, or psychiatric risk. It works statistically, sorting people by risk and showing up in correlations, but it is not destiny for any one person. It misses rare variants and interactions between genes. And because it is calibrated in one population, it often predicts worse in others.

## Where this leads

The hereditarian picture is simple once you accept the evidence. People differ, those differences run in families through genes rather than upbringing, and they are built from countless small effects that no programme can switch off. Polygenic scores will only get sharper as the datasets grow.

A politics that pretends otherwise will keep paying for interventions that can't work and blaming parents, teachers, and culture for gaps that were never theirs to close. Taking heredity seriously isn't cruelty. It's the precondition for policy that deals with people as they actually are.

You can deny biology, but you can't legislate it away.
