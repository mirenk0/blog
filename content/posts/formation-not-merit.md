The elite university was never meant to be a meritocracy. It was a place where young men were formed into a particular kind of person. Conservatives who now want to turn it into a pure test-score contest don't understand what they're destroying, or what will replace it.

## Putty, not prize-winners

Look at a future president's Harvard application from 1935. It's a handful of handwritten lines. He wants to go to Harvard because it offers "something definite", because his father went there, and because "to be a Harvard man is an enviable distinction." He never says what that something is. He never claims to have earned his place. He doesn't mention scores or say he beat the applicants beside him.

He didn't need to. The point of Harvard wasn't to reward the best test-taker. It was to take an eighteen-year-old who wasn't yet a Harvard man and make him one, through the professors, the clubs, the sports, the habits and manners of the old Protestant elite. The student was putty. The university was a finishing school, descended from the monasteries and seminaries it grew out of. That isn't meritocracy. It's inculcation, and it's a far better ideal.

## Diamonds in the rough

Even the tools that look meritocratic served that ideal. The SAT, now hated by the left, was introduced largely to find unrecognised talent in the Midwest and the South: the bright kid in Nebraska whose father hadn't gone to Harvard but who could still be formed into a Harvard man. It widened the pool. It didn't change the purpose.

Affirmative action came from the same logic. After the upheaval of the 1960s, segregation looked unstable, embarrassing in the Cold War, and a possible road to revolution. So the universities went looking for diamonds in the rough among black Americans too, and where the numbers didn't come out, they nudged them. A Supreme Court justice upholding the policy predicted it would be unnecessary in twenty-five years, because by then race would have dissolved into one American people. It hasn't. The gaps are as wide as ever. It hurts white applicants and it is unfair, but the motive is at least understandable. It was an attempt to build a broader elite for a whole society.

## A class, not a ranking

The key idea is the difference between a well-rounded student and a well-rounded class. An elite university isn't ranking individuals. It is choosing the people who will run the country, and assembling them the way a team is assembled: a physicist, an actor, a photographer, a violinist instead of a chess player. Admission to the top hundred or so universities means you have been chosen, and every student body is a choice.

That means luck has always mattered. The top tenth of any good state university could go to Yale and flourish. They weren't picked, that's all. Universities aren't a 100-metre sprint with a stopwatch, and they never have been. The same balancing already happens quietly for men, who get a thumb on the scale to keep the student body roughly half male. Nobody seriously wants a campus that is seventy per cent one sex.

## The ant farm

Affirmative action had already lost its legitimacy, and now the Supreme Court has struck it down. Conservatives cheer, because they hate the wokeness, the feminism, and the quotas, often with good reason. But look at what replaces it. Strip out both the old formation and the diversity regime, and you're left with pure credentialism: test-prep machines chasing a corporate job and a house in the suburbs, with no sense of duty to the society that trained them. An elite selected that way has no shared culture, no loyalty, and no idea what it's for.

Even the diversity regime kept something of the old institution alive. The black student in a Princeton sweater was still being inducted into Princeton, Presbyterian roots and all. A student body built purely on scores would keep nothing. It could also end up far less representative of the country, with fewer white students than the quotas ever allowed. A university that broadly resembles the nation it serves makes more sense than one captured by whoever games the test best.

## Where this leads

The question was never "who scored highest?" It was "what kind of people should lead us, and how do we make them?" A society that forgets the second question gets an elite of technicians with nothing to believe in. The answer is to bring back formation: universities that choose students for what they can become and then shape them into it.

A university should make gentlemen, not rank test-takers.
