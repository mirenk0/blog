The trouble with modern industry isn't the machine. It's that production now answers to nothing except price and speed. Arthur Penty saw this a century ago and proposed the one institution that ever held the line: the guild. A body of craftsmen that sets the standard of work, trains its members, limits entry so quality holds, and answers for the product. In that order of things, the trade governs the bench. The shareholder, the manager, and the market don't get the final word.

## A form of life

Industrialism is a way of arranging a civilisation, not just a set of tools. The factory does more than add machines. It separates the maker from the standard. Beyond a baseline of standardisation, only two commands remain: more, and faster. The craftsman's eye gives way to the manager's rate, and the knowledge of a man who has done the job for thirty years gives way to the knowledge of a man with a spreadsheet and a credential.

The guild once held that judgement. It has since been crowded out from two directions. The market undercuts it, and the state replaces it, setting standards from above through regulation written by people who have never stood at a bench.

## Property is not enough

Spreading ownership widely is the obvious cure for proletarianisation. The peasant had his own land, his own workshop, his own place. The wage labourer has none of it. But a smallholder in an unregulated market is just a small owner waiting to be bought out. He gets undercut by the big firm, or he takes a good offer and sells. Land reforms that hand out plots without any structure to protect them keep proving it: the land drifts back to the large and the well-connected.

So property needs a law around it. Title without craft law leaves a family exposed to the same price war that created the proletarian in the first place. Craft law without widespread title is just a committee governing proletarians under a new name. You need both. Medieval guilds show it can work: organised trades once held the legal authority to define quality, limit entry, and enforce standards, and they held it for centuries.

## The cheap and nasty

Anyone with a memory has watched things get worse. Christmas chocolates that used to be a treat now come in a plastic tub as a shadow of themselves. Crisps aren't what they were. Furniture comes flat-packed from a factory on the other side of the world. The cheap and nasty wins every time, and each remaining pocket of quality withers in the reign of quantity.

The exceptions prove the rule. After the video game crash of 1983, when the market drowned in unfinished junk, Nintendo saved the industry with a seal of quality: nothing reached its console without passing its own standard. It acted as its own guild, and it still refuses to be homogenised into everyone else. Well-made, expensive, carefully built hardware still exists. It just stands out because almost nothing else is like that.

Beauty is the test. When a civilisation can't make a decent chair, it won't make a decent street. "Beauty according to whom?" misses the point: beauty costs effort, and for a lifetime the direction of travel has been to cut it out. Ugly output means the standard has left the bench.

## Money from elsewhere

The factory chimney is only one enemy. The deeper one is money's claim to rule the workshop from somewhere else. What gets made is decided by whoever holds the capital, not by whoever holds the tools, and often not by the customer either. Woolworths didn't die because people stopped loving it or because it couldn't sell things. It died of credit. The asset-strippers who gut firms like it serve neither the producer nor the consumer.

Politics has the same flaw. Parliament represents the abstract voter: one man, one vote, stripped of occupation and craft, an interchangeable unit. A guild represents the concrete producer, answerable for a specific good. The citizen who makes nothing in particular can't defend the standard of anything in particular. He's just a consumer, which is exactly what a consumer economy wants.

## Where this leads

A guild only works in a place. Making, selling, and judging have to sit close enough together that shame and pride still operate. Within living memory, a family in a small town could be shunned for years over a minor transgression, and that is how communities policed themselves. Undercut your neighbour and everyone knew.

The costs are real. Things will be slower, scarcer, and dearer. We already accept that from a tailor or a jeweller. The City of London isn't going anywhere, and no guild will topple it. The task is to carve out life on a human scale alongside it, protected well enough that it doesn't get bought, lobbied, or regulated out of existence.

Either the trade governs the bench, or a bank a thousand miles away does.
