People are not interchangeable. Not as individuals, and not as peoples. Yet the dominant moral idea of the modern West assumes they are: that beneath language, history, faith, and custom there is one generic human, and that every difference is surface noise to be tolerated at best and erased at worst. Call it the ideology of sameness. It is the quiet premise behind most of what we now call progress, and it is wrong.

## Where it comes from

The idea has a long pedigree. Christian universalism declared every soul the same before God. The Enlightenment swapped God for Reason, the same faculty in every head, pointing to the same conclusions everywhere. Liberalism finished the job by reducing the person to a bundle of rights that could be carried anywhere and owed nothing to anyone. Each step had noble motives. Each step also pushed the particular further out of view, until the only thing left that counted was what everyone had in common.

## Equality is not identity

The trick is to confuse equality with sameness. Mathematical equality, where every unit counts as one and is swappable for any other, is the right tool for counting votes and the wrong one for understanding people. Proportional equality, giving each what fits their role, contribution, and situation, is what justice has meant for most of history. Chase the abstract version hard enough and you get injustice in practice: identical treatment of unlike cases, and resentment whenever reality refuses to come out even.

Real equality is reciprocity. It means mutual recognition between people and groups who are different and know it, and who value each other anyway. It affirms a common humanity without demanding a common mould.

## The abstract individual doesn't exist

Liberal theory starts from an individual stripped of every context: no family, no place, no language, no inheritance. That person has never existed. Humans are formed by the communities that raise them. We understand ourselves through our relationships, and we flourish through them or not at all.

Build a society on the fiction and you get exactly what we have. Tönnies drew the line between community, the organic solidarity of people bound by kinship, place, and shared memory, and society, an aggregate of strangers held together by contracts and markets. The move to mass society traded the first for the second. Loneliness, anomie, and the sense that nothing is owed to anyone are not side effects. They are what an atomised society produces when it works as designed. Nobody needs to romanticise the village to see that something vital was lost, and that getting it back matters more than most of the problems we spend our politics on.

## The universal runs through the particular

The usual objection is that defending particular identities means abandoning universal values. It's the other way round. Nobody reaches the universal directly. You reach it through a specific language, a specific literature, a specific set of rites and loyalties. The Greek tragedians speak to everyone precisely because they were so thoroughly Greek. A man with no culture of his own doesn't become a citizen of the world. He becomes a consumer of whatever the world is selling.

This is the mature form of identity: every people has equal dignity, and that dignity is the reason to preserve the differences between them, not to dissolve them into one global monoculture. Diversity in the real sense means distinct peoples staying distinct, not every city on earth turning into the same mixture.

## Unity without uniformity

The political answer is neither the centralised nation-state nor the rootless cosmopolitanism that wants to abolish borders altogether. It is federalism and subsidiarity: nested, overlapping communities, each deciding what it can decide for itself, with larger units handling only what smaller ones can't. Family, town, region, people, civilisation, every level real and none of them absorbing the rest. This is the European New Right's answer to the old question of how to hold unity and diversity together: let the whole be made of parts that stay themselves.

Globalisation promises a world where everyone is free because everyone is the same. The better promise is a world worth exploring because no two places in it are alike.
