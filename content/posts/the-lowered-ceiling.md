Democracy's problems aren't only institutional. They go down into the language. We traded depth and expressive power for breadth and accessibility, and the price of that trade was conforming to the linguistic level of the average person instead of the exceptional one.

## An instrument for the few

Classical Greek rhetoric was never meant for mass consumption. It was forged inside a tiny citizen body, perhaps thirty thousand men in Athens, who had the leisure, the education, and the stakes to master an extraordinarily demanding instrument. Its complexity wasn't a bug. It was a feature that rewarded long training and native brilliance.

The language could do things ours can't. Full inflection meant word order was free, so a sentence could hold its key word back until the last beat or set two ideas side by side for contrast. Particles marked every shift of emphasis, balance, and tone. Long periodic sentences built tension clause by clause and resolved it at the end, while prose rhythm made an argument move like music. Only the exceptional could use all of it at full strength. The greatest Athenian orator reportedly practised his speeches with pebbles in his mouth to perfect his delivery under strain.

## Built for scale

Modern languages, and above all global English, evolved under the opposite pressures: mass literacy, mass schooling, commerce, media, and communication across billions of people of every culture and ability. To work at that scale, they simplified. Inflection was flattened, word order was fixed, filler words multiplied, and unambiguous clarity beat layered nuance.

The result is a tool that almost anyone can use competently with moderate effort. Its ceiling is far lower. Brilliance at the Greek level was always rare, and even gifted people need years of immersion to approach it. A language designed so that everyone can take part has to be designed around those who can't reach that level, which means nearly everyone.

## The lowered ceiling

We haven't all become stupider. We've chosen a world in which language serves the many instead of exalting the few, and that choice has a cost. We have largely lost the ability to produce prose at Greek density and musicality, and increasingly the ability even to appreciate it. Modern attempts to imitate it, in translation or in purple prose, read as strained or pretentious, because the engine underneath isn't there anymore.

That matters beyond style. Language is the medium of thought. A culture whose shared instrument can only carry simple, flat statements will think in simple, flat statements, and its politics will follow. Slogans replace arguments because slogans are what the instrument does well.

## What we got instead

The trade wasn't pure loss. Plain, analytic prose turned out to be excellent for science, engineering, and law, where precision matters more than music. Mass literacy produced a global literature, and the same flattening gave us code, the most rigidly unambiguous language ever written. None of that was available to the Greeks.

But precision isn't depth, and breadth isn't height. A world that can write a perfect specification and can no longer write a great speech has gained something and lost something greater.

## Where this leads

The answer isn't to go back to ancient Greek. It's to deliberately cultivate linguistic instruments that demand more from us: forms that reward training, punish laziness, and raise the baseline instead of settling for the lowest viable denominator. Any movement that wants to escape materialism and cheap emotional rhetoric will need a language capable of more than either.

We chose equality of access over excellence of expression, and quietly lowered the ceiling on what collective human intelligence can reach. When a language lowers its demands, it lowers what it believes people are capable of.
