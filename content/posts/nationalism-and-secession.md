[Hoppe's](https://youtu.be/FALTTxOSD9s) main move is to separate two things we usually treat as one:

- **Political integration**: a government extending its power to tax and regulate over more territory.
- **Economic integration**: more people trading and dividing labour across more places.

The usual story says bigger political units mean bigger markets, and bigger markets mean more wealth. Hoppe says that confuses correlation with causation. Switzerland and Albania are both small, but only one of them is rich. The US and the Soviet Union were both huge, and only one of them had much of a market. Size alone doesn't tell you anything. What matters is how much a government takes.

## Why small states behave better

This is the part I found most convincing. A small government has close neighbours. If it taxes and regulates much more heavily than they do, its most productive people can just leave. Hoppe takes this to the extreme: could a father do to his household, or a mayor to his village, what the Soviet state did to its citizens? No. People would revolt or walk next door.

He uses this to explain why capitalism first took off in the most fragmented parts of Europe: the northern Italian city-states, southern Germany, and the Low Countries. Competition between lots of small rulers kept them in check.

The catch is that the freer states then tend to win wars and absorb their neighbours. As the winners get bigger and the remaining rivals get fewer and further away, leaving gets more costly, and the pressure to stay liberal fades. Taken to its end, in a single world government there's nowhere left to go. Hoppe reads the 20th century's steady growth in taxes and regulation, under a US-led order, as exactly that.

Small states also have every reason to embrace free trade. Russia could survive cut off from the world, thanks to its huge internal market. A single town couldn't. The smaller the territory, the more protectionism hurts, so the more open it has to be.

## Where I land

What I took away is that "nation" and "state" aren't the same thing. The nation is the people: their culture, language, and communities. The state is the machinery that taxes and governs them, and it often treats them as something to manage and draw on rather than serve. Nothing about the last hundred years in the West convinces me that more centralisation and bigger government have made nations more vital or more sure of who they are. If anything, it's the opposite.

So I find it odd when people who say they care about their nation oppose regions breaking away from rulers they never really chose. If you care about the people, their right to govern themselves at a smaller scale should be the obvious position.

I don't buy everything in the essay. Hoppe moves quickly from economics to big claims about culture and immigration, and those parts read more like assertion than argument. But his central point has held up better than the orthodoxy he was arguing against: competition limits power, and the more exits people have, the better they tend to be treated.

His closing picture is a Europe of hundreds of regions, cantons, and free cities like Monaco, San Marino, and Andorra, all competing for residents through low taxes, free trade, and sound money. It's utopian, sure. But it's a more interesting utopia than one world government.
