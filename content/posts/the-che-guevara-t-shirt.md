The pseudo-revolutionary influencers online aren't Che Guevara. They're the Che Guevara T-shirt: revolutionary imagery turned into a product and sold at a markup to people who want to feel like rebels. A streamer who calls himself a voice of the working class from a multi-million-dollar house is a case study in how capitalism absorbs its critics and sells them back to their audience. He rarely seems to notice it's happening.

## A joke from Brezhnev's Moscow

Leonid Brezhnev led the Soviet Union from 1964 until his death in 1982. He came from a working-class family, joined the party young, and served as a political commissar in the Second World War, finishing it as a major general. He took power by organising the ouster of Khrushchev, who was pensioned off to a dacha to grow vegetables and dictate his memoirs.

Stalin had held power through terror. Brezhnev held it through patronage. Loyal officials were kept sweet with cash, cars, dachas, and jewellery, and friends and relatives were promoted regardless of competence. His daughter Galina was caught up in a diamond-smuggling scandal. Brezhnev himself collected luxury foreign cars and piled up more than a hundred medals and titles, four of them Hero of the Soviet Union, all awarded by institutions he controlled. By the end of his rule, few people in the party seemed to believe in communism any more, and a growing share of the public plainly didn't either. The shelves were empty.

A Soviet joke from those years captures it. Brezhnev brings his mother to Moscow to show her how far he's come: the Kremlin office, the limousine, the helicopter over his hunting grounds, the dacha, the villa on the Black Sea, the cars. She says almost nothing. Finally he asks what she thinks. "It's all very nice, Lyonya," she says. "But what will you do if the communists come back?"

## Selling the revolution

The genre has its own advertising. Picture a fashion-magazine shoot in which a young online radical declares that the Kardashians are lying to you, that culture used to be the backbone of the resistance but now only sedates the masses, and that the revolution will not be televised, so take it to the streets. The copy calls him an antidote to clickbait culture with a bulletproof political ideology, interrogating the meaning of culture in a post-internet age. All of it is a fashion shoot, posted online. That isn't a parody. Shoots like it have run.

Then the products get bigger: the house in the hills, the sports car, the designer clothes, all while insisting they live on a basic budget. Ask about the house and the standard retort is mockery. So socialism means no iPhone? No soda? Can a socialist not own anything? Better a rich socialist, they say, than a broke kid defending capitalism.

That reply knocks down an argument nobody serious makes. Nobody is saying a socialist can't own a phone. The objection is to a particular kind of spending: buying things whose main purpose is to show that you're above the people you claim to speak for. To see why that matters, it helps to ask what makes something a luxury in the first place.

## What makes a luxury

Saffron is the world's most expensive spice. Second is usually vanilla, the flavour so ordinary that "vanilla" has come to mean plain and default. The orchid is native to Mesoamerica, but Madagascar became its great producer. The vine takes three or four years to flower, each flower has to be pollinated by hand on the one day it opens, and the beans then take months to cure. Real vanilla is expensive because it's hard to grow.

Vanilla *flavour* stopped being a luxury for a different reason. German chemists synthesised vanillin, the main aromatic compound, in the 1870s, and by the 1930s it was being made cheaply from the waste of wood-pulp mills. Synthetic vanillin lacks the hundreds of minor compounds in a real bean, but in a cake most people can't tell the difference. Once anyone could have the taste, it stopped signalling anything. Real beans are still costly. Nobody photographs themselves eating vanilla ice cream to show off.

Madagascar's own history adds an irony. From 1975 Didier Ratsiraka ran the country under "scientific socialism", with nationalisations and price controls. The economy went into decline, skilled workers and capital left, and by 1991 strikes and mass protests led by unions, churches, and students forced him to accept multiparty elections. The people who brought it down were not wearing designer Che shirts. Hardly anyone abroad noticed. That revolution really wasn't televised.

The lesson of vanilla is that luxury isn't a property of the thing. It's a property of access. A luxury good is usually well made, but its real job is to put distance between the owner and everyone else, and anything everyone can have can't do that. Polo shirts show it in miniature. A Ralph Lauren or Lacoste shirt is better made than a supermarket T-shirt, but not ten times better. Most of the price pays for the little horse or the little crocodile, which say you're comfortably off. For a few hundred more, Paul & Shark swaps the crocodile for a shark, so its wearers can look down on the crocodile people. And all of that is entry level next to Gucci, Prada, and Cartier.

## Showing it off

Thorstein Veblen named this in 1899: conspicuous consumption, buying expensive things in order to be seen buying them. He also noticed the odd economics that follows. Normally a higher price lowers demand. For status goods it can raise demand, because the price is the point. An $11 ring shaped like a twisted nail and a Cartier one look much the same. Only one of them tells people what you paid.

Everyone consumes. First come necessities, the groceries and rent of a genuine basic budget. Then there's discretionary spending, money spent purely for pleasure. Conspicuous consumption sits at the far end. It's the end the pseudo-revolutionary influencer lives at, while presenting himself as the voice of people who live at the other one.

Fidel Castro is a useful contrast. According to his former bodyguard, Castro had a private island and a yacht while ordinary Cubans queued for rations. But he hid it. He understood that a revolutionary leader flaunting wealth in front of hungry people wouldn't last. Conspicuousness is a choice, and the influencer makes it on camera.

None of this means owning nice things is wrong. Jay Leno has an enormous car collection because he loves cars and knows them inside out, not to impress anyone. Material goods give people real joy and meaning. A healthy appetite for them is compatible with having other values and caring about how things are made. Strict anti-consumerism, like other demands to abstain from natural appetites, tends to produce guilt and self-righteous misery rather than liberation. The cheerful middle-aged collector is usually happier than the anti-capitalist essayist inventing excuses for enjoying the films and games the market made for him. The problem isn't consumption. It's selling yourself as the enemy of the thing you're performing.

## The rebel sell

Joseph Heath and Andrew Potter made the larger argument in *The Rebel Sell* (2004). Everyone hates consumerism, everyone is a rebel against mass society, and yet consumer culture carries on untouched. Their explanation is that the critics misread what consumption is about. It isn't conformity. Apart from teenagers copying the cool kids, most people buy things to stand out. Cool means distinction, and for some people to be cool, others have to be uncool. Counterculture rebellion is just another round of that competition. It sells new products and threatens nothing, which is why capitalism happily stocks the Che shirt. As they put it, the Beastie Boys called everyone's bluff long ago: we're fighting for the right to party.

That also inverts the usual complaint. A planned economy offers a narrow range of goods and enforces the sameness critics blame on markets. Markets offer enough variety for everyone to consume differently, and they keep producing new things for each crowd to signal with. The rebel consumer thinks they're resisting the system while being one of its best customers, and the influencers sell to them directly: their own anti-capitalist slogans printed on merchandise at market prices.

The pose fails exactly where it would cost something. Ask one of them what he'd do if an elected socialist government actually voted to expropriate his house and hand it to the people, and the answer is that it's so far in the future there's no point arguing about it. What matters now, he'll say, is some popular policy like socialised medicine. But nobody asked about healthcare. They asked about his house.

So the charge of hypocrisy is only half right. Measured against what he preaches, the pseudo-revolutionary influencer is a hypocrite. Measured against how communism has actually been practised, he fits a long tradition. The revolution's spokesmen have usually lived well, from Castro's island to Brezhnev's garage, while explaining equality to the people outside. Orwell, a democratic socialist himself, wrote the line for it: all animals are equal, but some animals are more equal than others. What sets the influencer apart is that he does it openly, in a country where the communists never came, and so has no reason to wonder what he'd do if they did.
