The generation that complains loudest about socialism is the biggest beneficiary of it. Ask Boomers on Social Security whether they'd raise taxes on the young to keep their benefits, and a recent survey says 89% would. That isn't conservatism. It's luxury communism for the over-sixty-fives, paid for by their grandchildren.

## Socialism for me

Older conservatives warn constantly about the rise of socialism among the young. They have a point: the young flirt with it openly. But the young are only talking about it. Their parents are living it. Social Security and Medicare are the largest redistribution programmes in the country, and they move money in one direction, from people who are working and struggling to buy a house to people who already own one.

The benefits get treated as a sacred promise, as if they were made thousands of years ago and carved in stone. But a promise made with someone else's money isn't a promise. It's a claim on the people who weren't there when it was made.

## A pyramid with a shrinking base

Social Security was never a savings account. It's pay-as-you-go: today's workers pay for today's retirees. That worked when sixteen workers supported every retiree. Now it's fewer than three, and the number keeps falling. The young pay in at rates their grandparents never faced, knowing the fund is headed for insolvency before they can collect.

Insisting that the young pay more so the old can keep every cent isn't fiscal responsibility. It's one generation using its voting weight to tax another that has fewer homes, fewer children, and less of everything.

## What it replaced

Before the federal welfare state, Americans looked after each other. Fraternal benefit societies, mutual aid associations, churches, and charities ran an enormous network of insurance, sick pay, and burial funds. Your neighbour painted your fence and you watched his kids. Help was local, personal, and reciprocal, and it came with obligations to the community that gave it.

The New Deal state and the regulation that followed crowded all that out. Washington took over the job, the societies withered, and dependence moved from the neighbour to the government. The Boomers' grandparents, who built and ran those societies, would despise what their grandchildren have turned it into: not mutual aid but a demand, enforced by the IRS, that strangers decades younger pay up.

## Last off the Titanic

The Boomers want to be the last ones off the Titanic. It's a ship their parents and grandparents built, with the industry, the institutions, and the cheap housing and stable families they inherited. The Boomers steered it into the iceberg: the debt, the deficits, the housing they priced out of reach, the birth rate that leaves nobody to pay the bills. Now they want the lifeboats too.

## Where this leads

No system can survive a generation that consumes what it was meant to pass on. The way out isn't to keep feeding a scheme whose arithmetic is already dead. It's to end the pretence that benefits are owed whatever the cost, give the young some way out of a system that will never pay them back, and rebuild the local institutions that once made help a matter of community rather than taxation.

The Boomers didn't inherit socialism. They voted it in for themselves and sent the bill to their grandchildren.
