The West didn't lose its industry by accident. It gave it away, because its economists stopped being able to tell the difference between making money and making things. GDP counts a casino and a steelworks the same way. The real world doesn't.

## Not all money is equal

The classical economists, Adam Smith and David Ricardo, and after them Karl Marx, drew a line that modern economics has erased: productive versus unproductive labour. Productive labour leaves something behind, a physical commodity, a machine, an addition to the stock of capital. Unproductive labour vanishes as it is performed. Smith's example was the opera singer, whose song disappears into the air the moment it's sung. The same goes for the servant, the courtier, and most of what we now call services.

The neoliberal consensus threw that distinction out. Every pound earned counts as output, every transaction feeds the GDP figure, and a country's "competitiveness" is judged by how much money moves through it. On those terms, a casino is as good as a factory. It isn't. A casino generates revenue, but it adds nothing to what a country can actually do. The Marxist economist Paul Cockshott has spent years arguing for bringing the old distinction back, and the last few decades bear him out.

## The sanctioned and the open

Compare two countries on opposite paths. Iran has been under American sanctions in some form since 1979. Cut off from the "global community", it had to build what it couldn't import. Its natural gas output has climbed for decades. It builds its own cars, and the industry began with a licensed British design, the Hillman Hunter, which Iran kept making long after Britain stopped. It turns out engineers at a rate per head that most Western countries can't match, because a country that has to make its own things needs people who know how.

Britain went the other way. North Sea gas output has fallen steadily since its peak at the turn of the century. Car plants have closed one after another, and since the 1980s the manufacturing base has been sold off and hollowed out. Its universities, which once trained engineers from all over the world, Iran included, now send students into soft subjects instead. An ambitious young Iranian engineer used to come to Britain to train. Now they train at home.

## The hollowing out

That's the pattern across the neoliberal world. Economies become ever more service-based, built on financial flows rather than fixed capital, and they look rich on paper while losing the capacity behind the numbers. Meanwhile the countries the West sanctions or isolates are forced into autarky, and autarky forces them to keep the factories, the machines, and the know-how to use them.

Russia is the clearest case. It went neoliberal in the 1990s, then under Putin tamed its oligarchs, and under sanctions turned back toward self-sufficiency. Western commentators pointed at its modest GDP and laughed. Then it outproduced the entire Western alliance in artillery shells. GDP said one thing. The factories said another, and the factories were right.

## Capacity can't be bought overnight

The neoliberal answer is that money is just factories in potential: if you need industry, you can buy it. That's false. A reasonably complex plant takes five to ten years to build once planning and permits are cleared. Then it needs engineers and skilled workers, and they take a generation to train, by people who must already exist. Supply chains have to be rebuilt link by link. Every attempt at Western reshoring, Trump's tariffs included, runs into the same wall: you can't buy back decades of lost know-how with a budget line.

Neoliberal economists will say this is manufacturing fetishism, and that it ignores finance, services, and the subtle role of capital. The argument would be stronger if the results weren't so plain. The countries that kept their productive base are punching above their weight. The ones that traded it for financial services are discovering what they can no longer make.

## Where this leads

The way back starts with measuring the right thing. A serious country counts what it can build, mine, refine, and repair, not just what it can bill. It trains engineers before it trains managers. It treats energy and industry as strategic assets, not as legacy sectors to be offshored for a better quarterly figure. And it stops letting economists who can't tell a casino from a steelworks set its policy.

GDP measures money. Wars and crises measure what you can make.
