I spent some time at Yale, not long enough to belong to it and long enough to see how it works. From the outside it looks like a place that finds the brightest people and sharpens them. From the inside it looks more like a place that finds people who are already polished and keeps them comfortable.

## The postcard

The postcard version is true. The first snow on Old Campus really does stop people mid-step. The dining halls look like churches, the residential colleges give you a crest and a family before you've done anything to earn either, and there's a tradition for every week of the calendar. The classes can be absurdly good. You walk into a seminar of twelve people with someone who wrote the book, and nobody acts like that's unusual. For the first few months it feels less like a university than a very well-funded summer camp.

## Being held

What I noticed next was how carefully you're held. Deans check in, advisors check in, older students check in. Every stumble has a soft landing already laid out. It's pleasant, and it's also strange. Nothing is allowed to go badly enough to teach you anything. Eventually you realise the place isn't really testing you. It's assuming you'll be fine, because the people it picks have mostly been fine their whole lives.

## Dressing down

The most interesting thing at Yale is how people handle money. The rich kids don't show it. They dress down, talk vaguely about where they're from, and treat summer in some other country as a normal thing everyone does. Meanwhile the students who actually have nothing work just as hard to look like everyone else, and some of them carry that quietly for years. Everyone is performing a class they don't belong to, and almost nobody says so out loud.

## The tired ones

Under all of it is exhaustion. People say yes to everything because saying no feels like wasting the ticket. Clubs, societies, internships, the right dinners, the right people. By the end of term you can see who's running on fumes, and they're usually the ones who looked most composed at the start. A lot of very capable people walk around there privately sure they were the admissions mistake.

I don't regret going. The best hours I had there were late ones, in a common room with two or three people who had stopped performing for the night and were just talking about what they'd read. No tradition produced those hours. They happened in the gaps the schedule left, and I suspect that's where most people's real Yale took place.
