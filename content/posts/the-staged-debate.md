Britain has been debating immigration for more than half a century, and the debate has had essentially no effect on immigration policy. That isn't a failure of the debate. It's what the debate is for. Watch any panel show from the 1970s, then one from last week. The faces have changed and the venues have moved online, but the format is identical: a host, two sides, a round of talking points, and an invitation to come back next time. The endless debate never ends, because ending was never the point.

## The roster

Pay less attention to what is being said and more to who is allowed to say it. The people in the slots are almost always drawn from a small, recycled roster, a family of faces. Rees-Mogg, Braverman, Goodwin, Kisin, Hitchens on one side, Sarkar, Mason and their equivalents on the other. Like wrestlers on a promotion's books, they can swap storylines, change positions, and rotate in and out as the news cycle demands. What matters to the business is that whoever fills the slot is already on the roster. Random members of the public don't get the mic.

Notice too how the argument shifts once it reaches the approved venues. A raw public concern about demographic change arrives on the BBC already reframed: the issue becomes the difference between an English ethnic identity and a British passport. That distinction works as a safety net. It lets regime-aligned figures, who agree with the establishment on Ukraine and nearly everything else, play the "ethnic realist" in a debate whose limits are set before it starts.

## Getting to the front of the queue

Online, people celebrate whenever a talking head repeats something they said first. They think they've shifted the Overton window. Watch the mechanics instead. First comes a research phase: the media figure interviews the fringe character, or co-writes a report with some think tank. Then, when the storyline goes live, they say something slightly shocking, enough to be called racist and generate heat. That makes them the frontman for the position, so they now own it. Nobody asked them to represent you. They put themselves at the front of the queue, and from then on the watered-down version of your view is theirs.

New faces can be drafted in this way. A dissident gets elevated into the character people must "support", becomes the face of a position, and joins the roster. Getting organised only to produce another cable-news host isn't winning. It's recruitment.

## The debate is the message

The real function of staged debate isn't to settle a question. It's to get you used to the thing being debated. Years of conservatives arguing about whether a trans woman is a woman mostly taught conservatives to live in a world where the question is normal. A two-hour radio phone-in where every caller rages against digital ID still leaves one takeaway: digital ID is coming. A disgraced politician says something outrageous, every commentator reacts, and a week later he's on a major American show talking about war readiness. Whether you loved or hated him is irrelevant. The message that landed was that Britain is getting ready for war.

Many debates are also pushes at an open door. Climate activism asked governments to do what every government since Thatcher was already doing, only "more". Ukraine coverage asks not whether to fight Russia but whether we're doing enough. When the system does need to correct course, say because immigration overshot even its own targets, it lets noise build in the opposite direction just far enough to justify a limited, temporary adjustment, never a U-turn.

## Rejecting the frame

What the media really produces is what Walter Lippmann called the pseudo-environment, the set of concerns that frames your reality. You can test this. Stop following the news entirely and see which stories still reach you: the front pages at the petrol station, the motorway billboard flashing headlines for a talk-radio station, the one figure your feeds insist you notice this week. Whatever gets through your defences is what they needed you to know.

The same mechanism keeps reheating old outrage clips, circulating ten-year-old footage as if it happened yesterday, until the camera's narrow focus becomes the whole picture of a country. You can make anywhere look like utopia or hell depending on where you point it.

So the useful move isn't to win the debate. It's to refuse it: refuse the two sides, the pre-made boxes, the issue-by-issue scoring, and speak in terms that can't be clipped, softened and handed back to you by someone from the roster. If what you say can't be co-opted, nobody can stand at the front of the queue pretending to be you.
