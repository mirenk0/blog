Civil wars don't start because people are poor or angry. Plenty of societies are both and stay at peace for generations. They start in one of two ways: a powerful group feels power slipping away, or a weak group finds that fighting pays. Almost every civil war is one of these, or both at once.

## The falling group

The more dangerous road is the first one. A group that used to run things, ethnic or political, sees that it is losing its hold, or is about to. Maybe it has been pushed out of government. Maybe demographics, new institutions, or a new constitution mean it soon will be. Either way, it now has a grievance that is concrete and personal: status it once had and is being denied.

What turns that grievance into war is a mismatch between strength and representation. A group whose military capacity exceeds its political power has both a reason to fight and the means to do it. It still has the officers, the weapons, the networks, and the habit of command, but it no longer has the seats or the offices. That gap gets closed one way or another. If politics won't close it, violence will.

This is why the moment of greatest danger is not when a group is oppressed but when it is being displaced. People who have never held power rarely start wars to get it. People who are losing power often start wars to keep it.

## The slow burn

The second road starts small. A handful of rebels with little capacity and a long list of grievances begin an insurgency. At first they're a nuisance. But they recruit, collect resentments, and take whatever openings the state gives them.

The state usually gives plenty. Counterinsurgency is blunt. Every raid, arrest, and reprisal deepens the divisions the rebels feed on, and turns neutral people into supporters. The insurgency grows because it is being fought.

Then the rebels start competing with each other. Several factions chase the same pool of recruits and the same claim to lead the cause, and the way to win that contest is to be more radical and more ruthless than your rivals. The extremists outbid the moderates, the factions fight each other as much as the government, and the whole thing hardens into full-scale war that nobody can easily call off.

## What this means

The two roads call for opposite warnings. The first says watch the powerful, not the powerless: a society is most fragile when a group with guns feels its political standing draining away. The second says beware the cure: heavy-handed repression is the fuel a small insurgency needs to become a large one.

Peace isn't the absence of grievance. It's keeping power roughly in line with strength, and not handing the radicals a reason to win the argument.
